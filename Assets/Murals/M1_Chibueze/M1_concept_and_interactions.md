### 1. The reading of the mural

The mural has two halves that most people read as one picture. On the left is a
cubist mask built from interlocking planes of ochre, blue and cream. On the right
are five historical figures — Yaa Asantewaa, Mansa Musa, Thomas Sankara, Julius
Nyerere and Winnie Madikizela-Mandela — set against a gold map of the continent.

The obvious AR treatment is to tap each face and read a biography. Every wall on
campus could take that treatment, and it is what the wall already does by itself;
adding a phone to it adds nothing.

This experience takes a different reading, and it comes from the masquerade
rather than from the museum. In the masquerade traditions of West Africa a mask
is not a disguise and it is not a likeness. It is a vessel. Hanging on a wall it
is wood and paint and nothing else. It becomes an ancestor at the moment somebody
puts it on and dances, and the person inside is deliberately not named — the
anonymity is the mechanism, not a side effect.

Read that way, the five figures are not the subject of the mural. They are the
mask's previous wearers. The wall stops being a monument and becomes a queue.

### 2. Why this concept rather than another

Three reasons, in order of weight.

It is argued from the painting itself. The mask is physically made of separate
painted planes, so breaking it into shards and lifting them off the wall is not a
visual effect imported onto the mural — it is the mural's own construction, taken
literally. Every shard that flies is a crop of the actual photograph of the wall.
Nothing in the experience is imported artwork.

It gives the five figures a relationship to the mask instead of sitting beside
it. They come out of the mask and go back into it.

It ends on the viewer rather than on a biography. The closing beat is the only
moment in the piece where the phone shows the person holding it something about
themselves, and it is the moment the argument lands.

### 3. The sequence

**Detection.** The phone recognises the wall. The painted mask dims in place, as
though the paint were draining out of the brickwork, while fourteen shards fade up
in exactly the positions they are painted in.

**Emergence.** Shard by shard, over about three seconds, the flat painting lifts
off the wall and settles onto a curved shell. Centre shards push furthest forward,
edge shards stay back, and each one tilts to lie along the curve. The result is the
same painting with depth: a carved mask hovering roughly 85 cm off the wall,
holding a slow idle turn.

**Interaction 1 — tap the mask.** It opens along its own fracture lines and the
five painted figures rise out of it and orbit. Tap any figure to bring it forward
and read who they were; the matching area of the real wall lights up behind it, so
the digital figure and the painted one are visibly the same person.

**Interaction 2 — drag.** Turn the mask. Whichever figure you are standing in
front of is the one lit on the wall, so turning the abstract object navigates the
real people.

**Interaction 3 — the wearing.** Once all five have been met, tapping the mask one
more time does not close it. The five fold back inside and go out. The mask turns
square to you, steps a little closer, and its paint drains away until only the
silhouette is left and the live camera feed shows straight through where a face
would be. The mask is visibly empty, and what is in the hole is the room you are
standing in.

**Tracking lost.** Content hides within half a second rather than freezing in
mid-air, the scanning prompt returns, and state is kept — re-acquiring the mural
resumes rather than restarting.

### 4. On-screen copy

**Start screen**
MURALS, IN MOTION — Point your phone at the mask on the wall and find out who has
worn it.

**The mask panel**
THE MASK

The left half of this mural is not a portrait. It is a form — a mask built from
interlocking planes of ochre, blue and cream.

In the masquerade traditions this comes out of, a mask is not a disguise and it is
not a likeness. It is a vessel. Hanging on a wall it is only wood and paint. It
becomes an ancestor at the moment somebody puts it on and dances — and the person
inside is never named. That is the point of it.

So this is not a monument to the five people painted beside it. They are its
previous wearers.

Open it. Meet all five. Then tap it one more time.

**The closing panel**
THE MASK IS EMPTY.

In the masquerade it was never a portrait. It is a vessel — inert on a wall, alive
only while somebody is inside it, dancing. The wearer is not named. That is the
whole point of it.

Yaa Asantewaa. Mansa Musa. Thomas Sankara. Julius Nyerere. Winnie
Madikizela-Mandela. Five people who put this on, gave it a body for a while, and
set it down again.

You are looking straight through it now.

This wall is not a monument. It is a queue.

### 5. What this adds against the rubric

Mural–digital integration: the dim-then-emerge beat means nothing digital appears
until the painted original has visibly given it up, and every emerging piece is a
crop of the wall's own photograph.

Interaction design: three distinct interactions on one object, each revealing
something the previous one did not, with the third gated behind the second so the
experience has an ending rather than just a loop.

Creativity and originality: the piece makes an argument about what the wall is for
and ends by implicating the person holding the phone, rather than narrating five
biographies.
